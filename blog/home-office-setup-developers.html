Home office setup for remote developers: chair, desk, monitor and the rest

A remote developer's home office is not a showroom — it is where you sit for eight or more hours, read small text, type all day and join calls. The setup that works is the one that keeps your back, neck and wrists comfortable through a long coding day, and lets you see your code, your terminal and your call at the same time without hunching over a laptop screen.

This guide covers the pieces that matter, in the order that matters: chair first, then desk, then monitor, then the keyboard, webcam and lighting around them. It is for developers working from home who want a setup that lasts, not a shopping list of gadgets.

What you actually need

  • An ergonomic chair you can sit in for eight hours. Adjustable height, lumbar support and a seat that does not go flat. This is the piece your body touches all day — it comes before everything else. Browse ergonomic office chairs on Amazon →
  • A desk that fits your equipment and your height. Enough surface for a monitor (or two), a keyboard and a notebook. A standing desk lets you alternate sitting and standing, but a fixed desk at the right height beats a motorised desk that is too low. Browse standing desks on Amazon →
  • A monitor at eye level. The top of the screen should sit at or slightly below eye level. For most developers that means a 27-inch monitor, or two, on a monitor arm or riser — not a laptop screen looked down at all day. Browse 27-inch monitors on Amazon →
  • A keyboard and mouse that keep your wrists straight. A full-size external keyboard and a mouse that fits your hand, on a surface at elbow height. A wrist rest helps if your desk edge is hard. Browse mechanical keyboards on Amazon →
  • A webcam and lighting for calls. A laptop webcam points up your nose in bad light. Step by step

  1. Start with the chair. Sit in it for a full workday before you buy anything else — if your back complains, no monitor upgrade will fix it.
  2. Set your desk height so your forearms are parallel to the floor when typing, usually 28–30 inches for sitting. Feet flat on the floor, thighs parallel.
  3. Raise the monitor so the top third of the screen is at eye level, using a riser or a monitor arm. If you use a laptop as your main machine, a laptop stand plus an external keyboard does the same job.
  4. Add a second screen only once the first one is positioned right. Two badly placed monitors are worse than one well-placed one.
  5. Fix the lighting last: a light in front of you for calls, and no bright window directly behind you. A monitor light bar lights the desk without glare on the screen.
  6. Buy in stages if the budget is tight: chair first, then monitor position, then desk, then peripherals. Each step should make the workday noticeably better on its own.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying the chair last. The chair is the only piece your body touches for the whole day. A cheap chair with a premium monitor is the wrong way round.
  • Working from a laptop screen at desk height. Looking down at a laptop for eight hours strains your neck. A riser or a monitor arm costs little and fixes it completely.
  • Choosing a desk that is too small. A monitor, a keyboard, a notebook and a coffee need more room than you think. Measure your equipment before you buy the desk, not after.
  • Ignoring cables and clutter. A tidy desk is not about looks — cables underfoot and a crowded surface slow you down and make the space unpleasant to sit in. A cable tray and ten minutes of tidying fix most of it.
