Agency website that you can edit yourself: portfolio, services, inbox

An agency website should win work, not create work. Yet many agencies end up with a site only a developer can change: a new project goes live weeks late, a service price stays wrong because nobody wants to touch the code, and a contact form sends enquiries into an inbox nobody checks. An agency portfolio CMS fixes the real problem — it lets the agency itself update the site, the same week the work happens.

This guide is for agencies, studios and freelancers who sell services and need a website that shows their work, explains their services, and turns a visit into an enquiry. If your site still needs a developer for a simple text change, it is slowing your sales down.

The test:
Can someone on your team add a finished project, update a service page and reply to an enquiry — all in one afternoon, without touching code?

What you actually need

  • A portfolio you can update yourself. Add a project with images, a short description and the services used, and have it appear in the right category. Old work should be easy to hide, not left up because removing it is too hard.
  • Service pages that sell, not just list. Each service gets its own page: what it includes, who it is for, and what happens next. A visitor should understand the offer without needing a sales call to decode it.
  • An enquiry inbox, not just a form. Every message lands in one place with the service the visitor asked about attached, so nothing gets lost between an email inbox and a chat app.
  • Editing without code. Text, images, prices and project order should change from an admin screen. If only one person in the company can edit the site, the site will always be out of date.
  • Speed and mobile first. Most clients will first open your site on a phone. If the portfolio loads slowly or the form is hard to use, they move on.

Step by step

  1. List your services as clients see them — three to six offers, each with a plain name and a one-line outcome. Drop the internal jargon.
  2. Pick your six strongest projects and write each one the same way: the problem, what you did, and what changed. Honest detail beats a gallery of logos.
  3. Build one page per service, each ending in the same enquiry form. A visitor who reads about a service should not have to hunt for how to contact you.
  4. Set up the enquiry inbox before launch: who replies and how fast. A form with no owner is worse than no form.
  5. Give two people editing access and show them how to add a project, change a price and publish a service update. Test it with a real change, not a demo.
  6. Add a simple routine: every finished project gets a portfolio entry within a week, and every quarter you remove or refresh the weakest entries.
  7. Check the site on a phone every month — portfolio images, service pages and the enquiry form — because that is how most new clients will first see you.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A portfolio that never changes. A site showing only work from two years ago tells a visitor you have done nothing since. Fresh work is the cheapest proof you have.
  • Service pages with no next step. If a visitor finishes a service page and finds only a general contact link, many will leave. Every service page needs its own enquiry route.
  • Enquiries with no owner. A form that emails one person who is on leave is a lost client. Route enquiries to a shared inbox with a named backup.
  • Letting one person hold the keys. When only a developer can edit the site, updates queue up and the site drifts out of date. Editing must belong to the team that does the work.
