Donation website: campaigns, live progress and donor walls

A donation website has one job a fundraiser's social media post does not: a person who wants to give finishes giving in two minutes, from their phone, and can see their gift counted. Get that right and a small charity or community group collects from far beyond its own circle. Get it wrong and the donor stalls on a bank transfer instruction, a form that asks too much, or a page that never says whether the money arrived.

Most small charities still collect the old way: a post asking for help, a bank account number in the caption, and screenshots of transfers sent by message. It works until a donor mistypes the account number or simply gives up halfway. A donation website moves that exchange onto the screen: campaigns with a clear goal and deadline, a live progress bar that moves with every gift, preset amounts a donor can tap, and a donor wall that shows each gift counted. This guide is for charity founders and community organisers who want that loop, without building a fundraising platform from zero.

What you actually need

  • Campaign pages a stranger can understand in ten seconds. What the money is for, how much is needed, when it is needed by, and what happens with it — written plainly.
  • Live progress that updates with every donation. A progress bar and a raised-so-far figure that move the moment a gift lands. Donors give more when they can see the goal getting closer.
  • Preset amounts and a custom amount. A few sensible choices — small, medium, generous — plus a box for any amount. A donor should never have to decide what a "normal" gift is.
  • A donor wall that shows every gift counted. Names (or "anonymous") with amounts and messages, newest first. Seeing others give is what turns a visitor into a donor.
  • A donation flow a donor can finish alone. Pick a campaign, pick an amount, pay, get a receipt — in two minutes, on a phone, with no account creation and no message to send afterwards.
  • A back office that shows what matters today. Which campaigns are close to their goal, which have stalled, and who gave what — so thank-yous go out and no donor is forgotten.

Step by step

  1. Write one campaign at a time: a plain goal, a real deadline, and one page per cause — a school roof, a medical fund, a winter blanket drive. One page per cause beats one page for everything.
  2. Set the goal honestly and show the progress bar from the first gift. A campaign that sits at zero for a week looks abandoned.
  3. Offer preset amounts that fit your donors: small enough that nobody hesitates, large enough that a generous donor is not capped. Always keep a custom amount box.
  4. Put the donor wall on the campaign page itself, newest gift first, with an anonymous option. People give when they see others giving.
  5. Send a receipt and a thank-you the moment the payment clears — automatically, not when someone finds the time. A donor who is thanked gives again.
  6. After the first campaign, look at the numbers: which amounts donors actually pick, which campaigns stall, where donors drop off before paying. That is your plan for the next campaign.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Asking for a bank transfer and a screenshot. Every extra step loses donors. If giving means leaving your site, opening a banking app and messaging a screenshot, most people will mean to do it later and never do. Keep the payment inside the flow.
  • A goal with no progress shown. If a visitor cannot see how much has been raised or how close the campaign is, there is no reason to give now instead of later. Show the number and the bar, and keep them live.
  • One vague page for every cause. "Donate to our charity" tells a donor nothing. A page for one named cause, with one goal and one deadline, is what gets the gift.
