Event ticketing website: tiered tickets, attendee lists, organizer dashboard

Selling event tickets in messages and spreadsheets works until the night the early-bird price was supposed to end, someone pays the old price anyway, and nobody knows how many seats are left. Once more than a handful of people are coming, manual ticket sales turn into a second job.

An event ticketing website takes the whole flow off your phone: tickets a visitor can buy in a few clicks, prices that change on the schedule you set, a list of who is actually coming, and a dashboard that tells you whether the event is filling up or needs a push. This guide is for organizers who run events regularly and want the selling to run itself.

The test:
Can a visitor pick a ticket tier, pay, and get a confirmation to show at the door — while the attendee list grows without you touching a spreadsheet?

What you actually need

  • Tiered tickets that match how people buy. Early bird, regular and VIP are not decoration — they reward the people who commit first and give late buyers a reason to pay more. Each tier needs its own price, quantity and sale window.
  • Live availability, enforced by the system. A tier with twenty tickets left must show twenty and refuse the twenty-first sale. Overselling a room is the fastest way to turn a good event into a bad one.
  • An attendee list you can trust. Every paid order should record the buyer's name and contact details, and which tier they hold. At the door, that list is your check-in sheet.
  • An organizer dashboard. You need to see tickets sold by tier, revenue so far, and remaining capacity in one place — not by adding up messages at midnight.
  • Payment that confirms instantly. The buyer pays and immediately gets a confirmation with their ticket details. A payment that needs manual confirmation is a queue of worried messages.
  • An event page that sells the event. Date, venue, schedule, what is included and the refund policy — everything a hesitant buyer needs before they click buy.

Step by step

  1. Set the event facts first: date, venue, capacity and schedule. Capacity decides everything else — ticket numbers, tier sizes and when sales close.
  2. Design your tiers around real buying behaviour: a cheaper early-bird tier with a clear end date, a regular tier, and a VIP tier only if it includes something concrete, like reserved seating.
  3. Build the buying flow in order: pick a tier, choose a quantity, see the total, pay, and get a confirmation with a booking reference. Test it on a phone, because most buyers purchase on one.
  4. Set up check-in before the event: a way to mark each attendee as arrived, using the attendee list as the source of truth — not a printed list that is out of date before the doors open.
  5. After the event, look at what the dashboard tells you: which tier sold first and when sales spiked. That is your pricing plan for the next event.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Selling tickets without a hard limit. Taking money first and counting capacity later is how a room gets overbooked and a refund becomes a bad review.
  • Tiers that differ only in price. If VIP costs more but offers nothing extra, buyers notice and trust drops. Every tier should be worth its price on its own.
  • No attendee list at the door. If check-in depends on memory or on scrolling through payment messages, the queue grows and the event starts late.
  • Hiding the refund policy. If buyers cannot see what happens when plans change, many will not risk the ticket at all. A clear policy sells more tickets than a generous one nobody can find.
