Multi-vendor marketplace: vendor onboarding and storefronts

A multi vendor marketplace is one website where many sellers sell side by side: each vendor runs their own storefront, lists their products and manages their orders, while customers browse everything in one place and check out once. It is the model behind the big platforms — and it works just as well for a city, a niche, or a single category.

It is also the model most founders get wrong at the start: they build a shop, then try to bolt sellers onto it, and find it has no place for vendor accounts, no way to split an order across sellers, and no honest answer to the question every vendor asks first — when do I get paid, and how much is your cut? This guide is for founders who want a multi vendor marketplace with onboarding that works, storefronts sellers are proud of, and money that flows to the right people.

What you actually need

  • Vendor onboarding that a seller can finish alone. A vendor signs up, adds business details, uploads products and sets prices — without emailing you for every step. If onboarding needs your help, you have a support queue, not a marketplace.
  • A storefront for every vendor. Each seller gets their own page with their logo, products, ratings and policies, so customers can follow a seller they trust and vendors can send buyers straight to their shop.
  • One search, one cart, one checkout. Customers browse across vendors freely and pay once, even when the cart holds products from three different sellers. Splitting the order and the money is the platform's job, not the customer's.
  • Orders that reach the right seller. When an order lands, each vendor sees only their own items, with the details and delivery address they need. A vendor who cannot find their orders will not stay.
  • Commissions and payouts that are visible. Every sale shows the commission, the vendor's share and the payout status, so a seller never has to ask where their money is.
  • An admin view of the whole marketplace. Vendors pending approval, products waiting for review, disputes, payouts due and sales across the platform — one dashboard, so you stay in control as sellers grow.

Step by step

  1. Decide your niche and commission before you build anything. A marketplace for handmade goods in one city is easier to fill than one for everything, and a commission you can state in one sentence is easier to sell to vendors.
  2. Build vendor registration first: a signup form, a vendor profile, and an approval step where you check the seller before their products go live. Approving vendors keeps quality up and stops the marketplace filling with junk listings.
  3. Give each vendor a storefront page and a simple product form — title, photos, price, stock and delivery options. If a vendor can list a first product in ten minutes, onboarding works.
  4. Wire one shared cart and checkout that splits an order by vendor behind the scenes. Test it with two vendors: one payment from the customer, two clear orders for the sellers.
  5. Set the commission rule and payout schedule, and show vendors their balance and next payout in their own dashboard. Money that is visible is money vendors trust.
  6. Launch with five to ten vendors you recruited by hand, not fifty you never spoke to — ten active sellers look alive, fifty empty storefronts look abandoned.
  7. After the first month, check which vendors sell, which products get searched for and never found, and who needs help listing — that is your recruitment list for month two.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Letting anyone list anything. A marketplace with no vendor approval fills up with duplicate, low-quality or fake listings, and customers stop trusting it. Approve every vendor and review each first product.
  • Hiding the commission. If a vendor only learns your cut after their first sale, they will feel cheated. State the commission on the signup page and show it on every order.
  • One shared inbox for every vendor's orders. When all orders land in one pile, vendors cannot tell which are theirs, and customers wait while you sort it out. Orders must split automatically.
  • Launching with no sellers. A marketplace with no products is a blank page. Recruit your first vendors before launch day, and make sure their storefronts are full on day one.
